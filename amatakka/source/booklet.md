# **Amatakka: A Provisional Grammar**

*A teaching booklet for The Small Rain Down. Draft of 30 September 2026, revised the same day so that masuu is the verb "to be" for identity, mittuu is "to be, exist, endure", and the story past, distant future, habit and source words are settled. Provisional SRD layer (Jockles \- SRD).*

## **Read this first**

This booklet teaches Amatakka as if it were a finished language, so that you can learn it, write it and check it. It is not finished. Every rule is labelled. Rules that describe existing words carry the source tier of those words, and the other labels say what kind of rule it is. The four source tiers are:

> * **\[CANON\] is a word from fialleril or another direct Star Wars source book, as recorded in the Source column of the Amatakka Dictionary (the live Google Sheet). Where the Sheet's own note is unsure (for example "probably fialleril, not sure"), the word is marked CANON (unconfirmed).**  
> * **\[ESTABLISHED\] is a word already in the dictionary from earlier fanon sources (BLW, blue\_sunshine, skywvlked, @triscribe). A few entries have no source recorded and are listed here until they are checked.**  
> * **\[SRD\] is a word added to the dictionary as part of this language build (Source: Jockles \- SRD). That includes the Swadesh-list completion words and every word decided on 30 September 2026\.**  
> * **\[CANDIDATE\] is a word proposed during the language build without manual verification. It is on the "Candidates (review)" tab of the Sheet and is not yet in the dictionary.**  
> * **\[SPEC\] is a rule from the Amatakka Grammar** Specification and Dictionary Rule Audit (the PolyGlot-derived rules, as normalised on 29 September 2026).  
> * **\[DECIDED\] is a rule you decided on 30 September 2026\. \[NEW\] is a provisional proposal made for this booklet.** It follows the model in the spec: existing Amatakka first, then Tuareg, then other Berber, with Bantu substrate influence. Nothing marked \[NEW\] is canon until you accept it.

Tuareg and Berber comparisons in this booklet are written from recall, not from a checked grammar. Verify them against a reference (for example a Tamasheq or Tamahaq grammar) before treating them as sources. Vocabulary marked (candidate) is on the "Candidates (review)" tab of the Sheet and is still pending your review.  
**The big idea.** English is a subject-verb-object language with articles, a copula ("is"), tenses, and separate words for nearly everything. Amatakka is built on the Berber pattern instead. The verb comes early and carries the subject, the direction of the action and the object inside one word. The verb "to be" (masuu) can be left out in the present tense. Nouns change shape depending on their job in the sentence. Kin and body parts are never "just" a mother or a foot: they always belong to someone. Learn to expect a short, dense verb-word at the front of the sentence, and the rest follows.

## **Lesson 1\. Sounds and spelling**

**Rule 1.1 \[SPEC\]** The vowels are a e i o u y. Long vowels are written double (uu, ii, aa).  
**Rule 1.2 \[SPEC\]** The cluster *dt* is not allowed. (A check on 29 September 2026 found none in the dictionary.)  
**Rule 1.3 \[OBSERVED across all four tiers\]** The dictionary shows a consonant-heavy language with digraphs such as sh, kh, ch, ts, tz and the letter q. The full consonant inventory is not fixed, and this booklet does not fix it.  
**Rule 1.4 \[NEW\]** Where two rules put the same consonant side by side, write both (sessenuu, essenek \+ ker gives essenekker). Do not simplify. The one fixed exception is the numeral sorel "thirty" (sor \+ rel), a fused form written with a single r.

## **Lesson 2\. The verb "to be", and sentences without it**

**Rule 2.1 \[DECIDED\]** In the present tense the verb "to be" (masuu, see Rule 2.2) is folded into a describing word as an ending: \-ma for I, \-mu for you, \-mas for he, she or it. Matta "fine" \+ \-ma gives mattama "I am fine"; aleg "good" \+ \-mu gives alegmu "you are good"; washar "old" \+ \-mas gives washarmas "she is old". The ending already shows the person, so the pronoun is normally left out (Rule 2.3). The plural endings are \-mata we, \-matu you all and \-masen they (the third person keeps its s). The description comes first and the person last, so it reads like English "fine am I". The dictionary already shows it: mattama ek "I am fine" and ma wen? "what is that?"

| Amatakka | Literal | English |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Mattama ek. | well I | I am well. |
| Shatto spida. | fast speeder | The speeder is fast. |
| Migasha dol. | expensive food | The food is expensive. |
| Aleg spida. | good speeder | The speeder is good. |
| Mi wen? | who that | Who is that? |

**Rule 2.2 \[CANON \+ ESTABLISHED\]** The verb masuu is "to be" for identity and naming (revised 30 September 2026; the older glosses "I have, I stand for, I represent" are withdrawn). It is used for naming and identity, and for every tense except the present: *Ek masa nu ...* "I am called ..." (literally "I am named ..."). Use the short predicate-first form for present-tense qualities, and masuu for identity, naming and every other tense. Masuu is highly irregular (Rules 2.8 and 2.9). The present forms are masa (I am, canon), masu (you are, canon), mas (he, she, xe, it is), masta (we are), mastu (you all are) and masen (they are).  
**Rule 2.3 \[NEW\]** Because the verb already shows the person (Lesson 3), the pronoun is normally dropped. Add the pronoun only to stress it: *Essena.* "I know." / *Ek essena.* "I know (it is me who knows)."

**Rule 2.4 \[DECIDED\]** In every other tense the full verb masuu follows the description: Matta osa. "I was fine." Aleg os. "He was good." Matta hana. "I will be fine." Maqar hanen. "They will be big." Matta hanavor. "I will be fine, one day, after we are free." The short endings \-ma, \-mu and \-mas only ever mean "am, are, is" now.

**Rule 2.5 \[NEW\]** The verb mittuu is "to be, exist, endure" and is kept separate from masuu. Masuu says who or what something is; mittuu says that it exists, is present, or endures. It is built on mittu "endurance" and mitt-: for the enslaved, simply existing is endurance. It says "there is": Mit dol da. "There is food here." With gor "at" it says "to have": Mit spida gor=i. "I have a speeder" (literally "a speeder exists at me"). This is the Berber way of having something. The older gloss of masuu as "I have" is withdrawn. The forms are given under Rule 2.6, because mittuu is irregular.

**Rule 2.6 \[DECIDED\]** Mittuu is irregular, as the most common verbs in many languages are, and the part that is always kept is mit-. The regular forms would have been mitta, mittuh, mitti and mittek, with a banned triple t in the 1pl and 2pl. Instead the present is: 1sg mita, 2sg mituh, 3sg mit, 1pl mitta, 2pl mitru, 3pl mitek.

**Rule 2.7 \[DECIDED\]** Before a tense suffix on mittuu, these things change. (1) Final \-h and \-k drop before a consonant. (2) A single t between vowels at the suffix boundary becomes d, so \-tol becomes \-dol. (3) \-ker becomes \-ger after a vowel, in this verb only; every other verb keeps \-ker (gamaker). (4) \-qel never changes, because qel- is the root of the story tense. (5) The 3sg takes \-i- before consonant-initial suffixes, except \-tol, which gives mittol. (6) No triple consonants appear, and after t only t or r may follow.

Full conjugation of mittuu. Order: present, continuous (-tol), future (-ker), distant future (-bor), past (-nao), perfect (-nun), story past (-qel).

1sg: mita, mitadol, mitager, mitabor, mitanao, mitanun, mitaqel

2sg: mituh, mitudol, mituger, mitubor, mitunao, mitunun, mituqel

3sg: mit, mittol, mitiger, mitibor, mitinao, mitinun, mitiqel

1pl: mitta, mittadol, mittager, mittabor, mittanao, mittanun, mittaqel

2pl: mitru, mitrudol, mitruger, mitrubor, mitrunao, mitrunun, mitruqel

3pl: mitek, mitedol, miteger, mitebor, mitenao, mitenun, miteqel

The habitual prefix si- attaches as usual: si-mit, si-mita, si-mitru.

**Rule 2.8 \[DECIDED\]** Masuu is even more irregular than mittuu, because it is probably the most common word in the language, and it breaks the ordinary conjugation rules. It has a different stem for each group of tenses: mas- (present), han- (future and distant future) and os- (past, perfect and story past). The continuous lengthens the stem vowel (maasa) instead of taking \-tol. The future and past use their stems alone, with no \-ker or \-nao. The person endings are 1sg \-a, 2sg \-u, 3sg bare, 1pl \-ta, 2pl \-tu and 3pl \-en. The canon forms masa and masu are unchanged.

**Rule 2.9 \[DECIDED\]** Smoothing in masuu. (1) Final \-n drops before a consonant (hanen \+ vor gives hanevor; osen \+ qel gives oseqel; osen \+ nun gives osenun). (2) \-bor becomes \-vor in this verb only; every other verb keeps \-bor. (3) The 3sg perfect takes \-i- (osinun). (4) \-qel never changes. (5) No triple consonants appear, and t is only followed by a vowel. (6) han is spelled like the name Han on purpose, an in-joke in the way the source myth named leia and lukka. (7) Note for the orthography step: masa and maasa (and mita and mitta) differ only in length, so these contrasts must be kept clearly distinct when the spelling rules are set.

Full conjugation of masuu. Order: present, continuous, future, distant future (-vor), past, perfect, story past (-qel).

1sg: masa, maasa, hana, hanavor, osa, osanun, osaqel

2sg: masu, maasu, hanu, hanuvor, osu, osunun, osuqel

3sg: mas, maas, han, hanvor, os, osinun, osqel

1pl: masta, maasta, hanta, hantavor, osta, ostanun, ostaqel

2pl: mastu, maastu, hantu, hantuvor, ostu, ostunun, ostuqel

3pl: masen, maasen, hanen, hanevor, osen, osenun, oseqel

## **Lesson 3\. The verb: root, person, time**

**Rule 3.1 \[SPEC\]** A regular native verb is cited in *\-uu*. To conjugate, remove *\-uu* and add a person ending.

| Person | Ending | essenuu "know" | gamuu "sit" |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1sg (I) | \-a | essena | gama |
| 2sg (you) | \-uh | essenuh | gamuh |
| 3sg (he, she, xe, they) | \-i (provisional default) | esseni | gami |
| 1pl (we) | \-ta | essenta | gamta |
| 2pl (you all) | \-tu | essentu | gamtu |
| 3pl (they) | \-ek (provisional default) | essenek | gamek |

**Rule 3.2 \[SPEC\]** The third-person endings really depend on the last sound of the stem. The spec lists \-i, \-at, \-is, \-di, \-dat, \-dis (singular) and \-ek, \-et, \-es, \-dik, \-det, \-des (plural). Until those rules are tested, this booklet uses \-i and \-ek as the default. Treat every third-person form as provisional.  
**Rule 3.3 \[SPEC\]** Time and mood are added after the person ending. Amatakka has two tenses that lie outside ordinary time: \-qel for stories and legends, and \-bor for the distant future after we are free.

| Marker | Meaning | 1sg essena- | 2sg essenuh- |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| (none) | simple present | essena | essenuh |
| \-tol | present continuous | essenatol | essenuttol |
| \-ker | simple future | essenaker | essenuhker |
| \-bor | distant hopeful future ("after we are free") | essenabor | essenuhbor |
| \-nao | simple past | essenanao | essenuhnao |
| \-nun | perfect past | essenanun | essenuhnun |
| \-qel | story past (legends, "long ago") | essenaqel | essenuhqel |

**Rule 3.4 \[SPEC\]** After a final h, *\-tol* becomes *\-ttol* (essenuh \+ tol \= essenuttol). The 1pl story past uses \-qel like the others (essentaqel).  
**Rule 3.5 \[SRD decision\]** \-qel is the story past. It is the tense in which stories and legends are told, the tense of "a long time ago, in a galaxy far, far away". Its suffix is built on the root qel-, as in qelin "to tell a story" and qelinnu "story". \-bor is the distant hopeful future, "the future after we are free". It carries hope, but it is a very distant tense. Neither means "maybe" or "I guess". Ordinary talk about the near future uses \-ker, and ordinary talk about the past uses \-nao and \-nun. Using \-qel about your own recent life would sound like the start of a legend, and using \-bor about a small everyday thing would sound wistful. In writing it is always \-qel; in speech it is often softened to \-gel in some dialects (mitaqel may be heard as mitagel).  
**Rule 3.6 \[ESTABLISHED\]** Older inherited verbs are not always in \-uu (for example *ji* "see", *ku* "eat"). They are left as they are.

## **Lesson 4\. Word order**

**Rule 4.1 \[NEW\]** The default order is verb, subject, object (VSO), with the subject dropped when the verb already shows it. This is the usual Berber pattern and it fits the existing predicate-first sentences.  
**Rule 4.2 \[NEW\]** Descriptive words come after the noun, and so do numbers and the words for "this" and "that". Words for "all, many, few, some" come before it.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| spida shatto | a fast speeder |
| spida wen | that speeder |
| spida wai | this speeder |
| riivken top | two ships |
| spidan shatton sor | three fast speeders |
| kullu riivken | all ships |
| ijar spidan | many speeders |

**Rule 4.3 \[NEW\]** There are no articles. "A speeder" and "the speeder" are both *spida*. Use *wen* or *wai* when the difference matters.  
**Rule 4.4 \[CANON \+ ESTABLISHED\]** Compounds are mixed: some put the head first (*riya-chelik*), some put the possessor first (*banta-shihr*). Both stay valid. New compounds in this booklet put the head last for modifiers (*dol-ta*, *abara-spida*) as the candidates do.

## **Lesson 5\. Nouns: gender, number, and the two shapes of a noun**

**Rule 5.1 \[SPEC\]** Gender is optional and marked by a final suffix, used mainly on people: *\-ka* male, *\-ku* female, *\-ke* Gerian (fourth gender), *\-ki* nonbinary. A noun with no suffix is fine. Example: *amu-ku* "mother".  
**Rule 5.2 \[ESTABLISHED \+ SRD, generalised as NEW\]** The plural is *\-n* after a vowel and *\-ken* after a consonant. The dictionary shows *akka* to *akkan* and *ek* "I" to *ekken* "we". This booklet extends the same pattern to all nouns.

| Singular | Plural |
| :---- | :---- |
| spida "speeder" | spidan |
| riiv "ship" | riivken |
| dol "food" | dolken |
| kash "currency" | kashken |
| amu "mother" | amun |

**Rule 5.3 \[NEW\]** Adjectives agree in number: *spidan shatton* "fast speeders". When the noun carries a gender suffix and a plural, the order is root, gender, plural: *amu-ku-n* "mothers".  
**Rule 5.4 \[CANON, generalised as NEW\] The annexed shape.** Berber nouns have a "free" shape and an "annexed" shape, used when the noun follows a verb, a preposition, or another noun. The dictionary already shows this: *amu* "mother" (the nominative) becomes *ama* (the genitive). This booklet generalises it: a noun ending in *\-u* changes to *\-a* in the annexed shape; other nouns do not change. The annexed shape is used:

> * for the possessor after the thing possessed: *spida ama* "mother's speeder";  
> * for the subject after the verb: *Gami ama.* "Mother sits.";  
> * after a preposition: *nel ama* "from mother".

Most nouns will not visibly change, exactly as in Berber, where only some nouns show the difference. The rule is a small extension of the canon amu / ama. Say so explicitly when you use it.

## **Lesson 6\. Belonging: clitics and inalienable nouns**

**Rule 6.1 \[NEW\]** Short pronoun endings, called clitics, attach to the end of nouns, verbs and prepositions. One set does three jobs: it shows the owner of a noun, the object of a verb, and the person after a preposition. This is the Berber pattern of pronoun suffixes.

| Person | Independent | Clitic | Meaning of the clitic |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1sg | ek | \=i | me, my |
| 2sg | te | \=k | you, your |
| 3sg | eta (he), etu (she), ete (xe), eti (they) | \=t | him, her, xem, them, its |
| 1pl | ekken | \=nay | us, our |
| 2pl | ten | \=kun | you all, your |
| 3pl | eti | \=tan | them, their |

The independent pronouns are \[CANON \+ ESTABLISHED \+ SRD\]. The clitics are \[NEW\]. The clitics are gender-neutral: the gender of "him, her, xem" comes from context or from adding the independent pronoun.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| spida=i | my speeder |
| riiv=nay | our ship |
| Essena=t. | I know it. |
| Essenuh=i. | You know me. |
| nel=k | from you |
| ed=nay | with us |
| Kepi=i ama dol. | Mother gives me food. |

**Rule 6.2 \[ESTABLISHED \+ SRD\]** The prepositions are *a* "in", *ed* "with", *gor* "at, by", *keik* "about" and *nel* "from, of, given by".  
**Rule 6.3 \[NEW\] Belonging that cannot be cut loose.** Kin, body parts and names are inalienable. They are never spoken bare unless someone is speaking generally: you say *amu=i* "my mother", *adar=k* "your foot", *nu=t* "his name". A bare *amu* means "a mother, mothers in general". This is a strong Berber feature and it suits a culture where family is taken from you and belonging is not a thing you assume.  
**Rule 6.4 \[CANON \+ ESTABLISHED\]** The name phrase *ek masa nu ...* "I am called ..." remains valid as written. The dictionary also attests possessive pronouns: em "my, mine" and ema/eme/emi/emu "his, xir, their, her". The clitics in this lesson are a proposal that would sit beside these, and which set to use for possession is undecided (see Decisions for you).

## **Lesson 7\. Going, coming, and where you stand**

**Rule 7.1 \[NEW\]** Verbs take a direction ending that shows the action's direction relative to the speaker. *\=d* means toward the speaker (hither). *\=nn* means away from the speaker (thither). Berber languages mark direction on the verb in the same way.  
**Rule 7.2 \[NEW\]** Word order on the verb is: stem, time marker, direction, object. Example: *foreta=nn=t* "I fly away with it" (literally "I fly, away, it").

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| Argesha=d. | I walk toward you (toward here). |
| Argesha=nn. | I walk away. |
| Foreta=nn. | I fly away. |
| Kepi=d=i ama dol. | Mother hands me food (toward me). |

**Rule 7.3 \[SRD\]** The verb *asuu* "to come, arrive" already exists and keeps its meaning. Do not add *\=d* to it unless you want to stress the direction.  
Direction matters for a people who are moved, sold and taken. The language has a grammatical place for "toward me" and "away from me" on every verb.

## **Lesson 8\. Saying no, and saying yes**

**Rule 8.1 \[CANON \+ ESTABLISHED\]** The negative particle is *kel* (also *kol*, *koa*). *Kai* means "yes, agreed".  
**Rule 8.2 \[NEW\]** *Kel* stands before the verb it negates. Berber negation also comes before the verb.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| Kel essena. | I do not know. |
| Kel essenaker. | I will not know. |
| Kel aleg spida. | The speeder is not good. |
| Kel gam\! | Do not sit\! |

**Rule 8.3 \[NEW\]** Negative words are built with *kel-*: *kel-mi* "nobody", *kel-ma* "nothing", *kel-meni* "nowhere". The verb is still negated by *kel*: *Kel esseni kel-ma.* "He knows nothing."  
**Rule 8.4 \[NEW\]** A yes or no answer repeats the verb: *Essenuh?* "Do you know?" gets *Essena.* "I know." or *Kel essena.* "I do not know." *Kai* and *kel* alone are also fine.

## **Lesson 9\. Asking questions**

**Rule 9.1 \[SRD\]** The question words are *ma* "what", *mi* "who", *mai* "how", *meni* "where" and *menged* "when". They replace the thing asked about; they do not move to the front unless the word order already puts them there.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| Ma wen? | What is that? |
| Mi asi? | Who comes? |
| Meni te? | Where are you? |
| Menged asuh? | When do you come? |
| Mai essenuh=t? | How do you know it? |
| Meni nel te? | Where are you from? |

**Rule 9.2 \[NEW\]** A yes-or-no question is opened by the particle *is*, with rising pitch at the end: *Is gamuh?* "Do you sit?" Berber languages use a similar question particle.

## **Lesson 10\. Numbers and counting**

**Rule 10.1 \[SRD\] The numbers 1 to 10 are single syllables: tsi 1 \[ESTABLISHED\], top 2 \[ESTABLISHED\], sor 3, kuz 4, fus 5 (from afus "hand"), dis 6, zas 7, tum 8, tiz 9, rew 10 \[SRD, decided 30 September 2026\].**  
**Rule 10.2 \[DECIDED\] rew is the free form of ten; \-rel is its bound form in compounds. N-rel means N tens: top-rel 20, sorel 30, kuz-rel 40, dis-rel 60, zas-rel 70, tum-rel 80, tiz-rel 90\. sorel is a lexical fusion of sor \+ rel, written with a single r (the one exception to Rule 1.4).**  
**Rule 10.3 \[NEW\]** The number follows the noun. For 2 and above the noun is plural: *riivken top* "two ships". For 1 the noun is singular: *riiv tsi* "one ship".  
**Rule 10.4 \[SRD\]** *sedenuu* means "to count".

**Rule 10.5 \[CANON\]** efin 50 and dekka 100 are irregular and stay as they are; the regular forms fus-rel and rew-rel are not used. 1000 is rekkat \[SRD\], an irregular word that is not built from dekka or \-rel, in the way English "thousand" is separate from "hundred".

**Rule 10.6 \[DECIDED\]** Larger counts use thirties: N-sorel (top-sorel 60, sor-sorel 90, kuz-sorel 120, fus-sorel 150). Ages, days and close kin (up to thirty people) are counted in thirties; ordinary goods are counted in tens. 60 and 90 have both forms (top-sorel or dis-rel; sor-sorel or tiz-rel).

**Rule 10.7 \[DECIDED\]** The joiner \-e- "and" puts the larger number first: efin-e-tsi 51, rew-e-tsi 11, sorel-e-tsi 31\.

**Rule 10.8 \[DECIDED\]** Zero is kolak, the word for nothing or empty. Kolak is also used for zero in sums and counts.

**Rule 10.9 \[DECIDED\]** To say what place something has in a line (first, second, third), add \-an to the number: soran third, kuzan fourth, fusan fifth, sorelan thirtieth. Two are special: toba means second and ebdan means first (from ebduu, to start). The last one is ereiban.

**Rule 10.10 \[DECIDED\]** To say how many times, put toba after the number: tsi toba once, top toba twice, sor toba three times.

**Rule 10.11 \[DECIDED\]** Fractions: xe is a half and xexe is a quarter (half of a half). A third has its own word, sorxe, which is still provisional. Every other fraction is tsi plus the order word: tsi fusan a fifth, tsi disan a sixth.

**Rule 10.12 \[DECIDED\]** The year is ar-ewel, "the great turn" (ar- great \+ ewel a turn, from eweluu). It runs from one marokkepu, the day all three moons are full, to the next, about ten moon-rounds. Published Tatooine sources name no seasons, so the stretches are our own assumptions: araplu is the great drought, the long dry stretch; arail is the great mist, the humid build-up before the new year; arumak is the mighty storm, the sandstorm stretch after it. Each is ar- plus a shortened root (aplu from aplutu, ail from aila, umak from umakkar). The lengths are six, three and one moon-rounds (confirmed): araplu six, arail three, arumak one, ten in all. The old word asagas is retired.

**Rule 10.13 \[DECIDED\]** The month is sorlev, one round of the moons, about thirty days. It is the rounded-off form of sorel-lev (thirty moon) and is learned as one word. Ten sorlev make an ar-ewel. khepi means time or hour, and asagi means season, the general word for araplu, arail and arumak.

**Rule 10.14 \[DECIDED\]** A stretch of ten days is usanna, three to a sorlev. The day is split into morning, tagasu, and evening, tamettu; dawn and dusk stay haro, noon stays khepatag and night stays nittu. These three words are new and were shaped on Berber and Tuareg ideas without copying them: usanna from a Berber word for days, tagasu from a Tamasheq morning greeting, tamettu from a Kabyle word for evening.

Rule 10.15 \[DECIDED\] Days relative to now are made by adding a verb time ending to kashka (day): kashkalu today, kashkanao yesterday, kashkaker tomorrow. The same ending works for the far times: kashkabor is the day after we are free, and kashkaqel is once upon a time, the story-day.

## **Lesson 11\. Making new verbs from old**

Berber languages build whole families of verbs from one root using a few prefixes. Amatakka does the same. The prefix goes on the root, and then you conjugate as usual.

| Function | Prefix before a vowel | Prefix before a consonant |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Causative "make someone do it" | s- | se- |
| Reciprocal "each other" | m- | me- |
| Passive "be done" | tt- | tu- |

**Rule 11.1 \[NEW\]** Examples:

| Root | Causative | Reciprocal | Passive |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| essenuu "know" | sessenuu "teach" | messenuu "know each other" | ttessenuu "be known" |
| asuu "come" | sasuu "bring" |  |  |
| gamuu "sit" | segamuu "seat someone" |  |  |
| kepuu "give" |  | mekepuu "give to one another" | tukepuu "be given" |

**Rule 11.2 \[NEW\]** The passive names the doer with *nel* "given by": *Tukepi dol nel ama.* "Food is given by Mother."  
**Rule 11.3 \[ESTABLISHED \+ CANON\]** Existing affixes still work: *kari-* becoming, *kol-* without, *nar-* changed or opposing, *ar-* great, *\-i* small, *\-du* on purpose, *\-lu* now, *\-ta* place or agent, *\-kin* bringer. A doer is *root-ta*: *kep-ta* "giver".

## **Lesson 12\. Commands and wishes**

**Rule 12.1 \[NEW\]** The command to one person is the bare root: *Gam\!* "Sit\!" *Essen\!* "Know\!" This matches the inherited bare verb *ji* "see\!"  
**Rule 12.2 \[NEW\]** The command to several people adds *\-at*: *Gam-at\!* "Sit, all of you\!"  
**Rule 12.3 \[NEW\]** A prohibition is *kel* plus the bare root: *Kel gam\!* "Do not sit\!"  
**Rule 12.4 \[ESTABLISHED\]** *\-lu* means "now, may this happen now". Add it to a command for urgency: *Gam-lu\!* "Sit now\!"

## **Lesson 13\. Joining ideas: who, which, that**

**Rule 13.1 \[ESTABLISHED \+ SRD \+ CANON (unconfirmed)\]** Joining words: *\-e-* and, *ba* or, *pu* but, *kud* if, *felas* because, *menged* when, *elat* apart from.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| Kud gami ama, kel essena. | If Mother sits, I do not know. |
| Kai pu kel essena. | Yes, but I do not know. |

**Rule 13.2 \[NEW\] Relative clauses.** To say "the mother who sits", use the participle. Berber languages use a special verb form here that has no person ending. In Amatakka, replace *\-uu* with *\-an*, and put it after the noun and *wen*. The participle does not show the person: the noun shows who is meant.

| Amatakka | English |
| :---- | :---- |
| amu wen gaman | the mother who sits |
| amu wen kepan dol | the mother who gives food |

**Rule 13.3 \[NEW\] Stressing one part of a sentence.** To say "it is X that ...", put *wa* before X and use the participle: *Wa ek gaman.* "It is I who sit." This is the Berber way to answer "who?" and it makes the answer stand out.

## **Lesson 15\. Habit, and how you know it**

**Rule 15.1 \[DECIDED\]** The prefix si- marks what is done habitually, as a matter of custom. It comes from si "way, a way of thinking, religion, teaching", and it echoes the Mando'a idea of the Way, where custom and creed are one. It follows the same pattern as lev and lev-, a word and a prefix that share a form. It attaches to the verb, before the person ending, and combines with any tense.  
Examples: Si-gama. "I habitually sit." Si-gamuh. "You habitually sit." Si-gamanao. "I used to sit." Si-gamaker. "I will keep sitting (I will make it my habit)." Si-gama kullu nittu. "I sit every night, as a habit."  
**Rule 15.2 \[DECIDED\]** Three short words go at the end of a clause to say how you know something. Rap means "so I was told" (reported). Ji means "I saw it myself" (witnessed). Lev means "I feel it in my heart" (felt or intuited).  
Examples: Gami ama rap. "Mother sits, so I was told." Gami ama ji. "Mother sits; I saw it myself." Gami ama lev. "Mother sits; I feel it in my heart."  
**Rule 15.3 \[DECIDED\]** The source words are never required. Most sentences have none. They work as intensifiers or clarifiers: ji makes a claim firmer, rap shows that the speaker is passing something on, and lev shows a feeling and not a proof.  
**Rule 15.4 \[DECIDED\]** Rap, ji and lev are also foundational words for vocabulary about storytelling, learning and sharing information. The dictionary already has families around them: rap "mouth, tongue, words" (rappal, rappali, marstrap), ji (ji-kest) and lev (levadakka, levrukka). Possible new words, not yet in the dictionary and waiting for your review: rap-kin "messenger", ji-ta "witness", lev-rap "heart-words (a vow, lullaby or prayer)" and lev-ji "to sense".

## **How Amatakka differs from English**

| English | Amatakka |
| :---- | :---- |
| Subject comes first. | Verb comes first; the subject is often only inside the verb. |
| "is" links a subject to a quality. | The verb "to be" is masuu, and it can be left out in the present tense. The quality comes first: *Shatto spida.* |
| Articles (a, the). | No articles; use *wen* or *wai* to point. |
| Adjectives come before the noun. | Adjectives, numbers and "this, that" come after the noun. |
| "I", "you" are separate words. | Person is inside the verb; object and owner are short endings (=i, \=k, \=t). |
| "Come" and "go" are different verbs. | Direction (toward, away) can be added to any verb. |
| A mother is just a mother. | Kin, body and name are always someone's: *amu=i* "my mother". |
| Nouns do not change shape. | A noun has a free shape and an annexed shape (*amu* / *ama*). |
| Tense: past, present, future. | Continuous, future, past and perfect, plus a story past for legends and a distant hopeful future for the time after we are free. |
| Many separate words: "make someone know". | One prefix on a verb: *sessenuu* "teach". |
| Relative clause with "who, that". | A verb form without a person ending: *amu wen gaman*. |

## **Where each rule comes from**

Tuareg and Berber forms are from recall and must be verified before being used as a source.

| Feature | Berber / Tuareg pattern | Amatakka |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Word order | Verb first; predicate-first sentences | VSO, predicate-first (Lessons 2, 4\) |
| Annexed state | Free and annexed noun shapes | amu / ama, generalised (5.4) |
| Pronoun suffixes | Short suffixes for object, owner, after prepositions (for example \-i "me") | \=i, \=k, \=t (Lesson 6\) |
| Direction on the verb | Orientation particles: hither and thither | \=d, \=nn (Lesson 7\) |
| Participle | Verb form without person ending in relative clauses | \-an (13.2) |
| Derived verbs | Causative s-, reciprocal m-, passive tt- | s(e)-, m(e)-, tt-/tu- (Lesson 11\) |
| Negation | Particle before the verb | *kel* before the verb (established word, new placement) |
| Plural | Plural ending \-en (with variants) | \-ken / \-n (established and SRD pattern) |
| Kin possession | Kin terms take possessive suffixes | Inalienable nouns (6.3) |

## **Still open**

These parts of the language are not decided by this booklet, and no rule here should be read as settling them: comparison ("faster than"), how the source words rap, ji and lev combine with questions and negation, the full third-person endings and their rules, irregular verb forms in practice, the full consonant inventory and pronunciation, the writing system as used with these rules, the calendar and time words (week, month, day names), how masuu forms behave in questions and negation, and how the new clitics interact with the full set of inherited verbs.

## **Decisions for you**

> 1. Should the annexed shape cover the subject after the verb (5.4), or only the possessor (the canon genitive)?  
> 2. Do you want one clitic set for owner, object and preposition (Lesson 6), or separate sets, as in Berber?  
> 3. Do the direction endings \=d and \=nn stay (Lesson 7)?  
> 4. Is *is* acceptable as the yes-or-no particle, or should another form be used (9.2)?  
> 5. The plural rule (-n after a vowel, \-ken after a consonant) extends the established akkan and the SRD ekken. Keep it for all nouns?  
> 6. Is the inalienable class (kin, body, name) a rule you want in the language (6.3)?  
> 7. May masuu be left out in the present tense (2.1), or should it always appear?  
> 8. The dictionary attests the possessives em and ema/eme/emi/emu. Should possession use those instead of the clitics (6.4)?  
> 9. Settled: mittuu is irregular (Rules 2.6 and 2.7). Its 1pl is mitta and its 2pl is mitru, so the triple t no longer arises. The \-ger voicing stays specific to mittuu unless you decide otherwise.
