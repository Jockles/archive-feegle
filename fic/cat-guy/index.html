The thing nobody told you about the NHL was how quiet it got afterwards.

Not on the ice. On the ice it was never quiet: skates and sticks and Wyatt narrating his own saves, Bood yelling at the bench, twenty thousand people in red and black making noise at the same time. And not on the bus or the plane, where somebody was always playing music off their phone without headphones and somebody else was always complaining about it.

It was the apartment that was quiet. Luca let himself in at a quarter to two in the morning after a four-game road trip, dropped his bag in the hall, and stood there in the dark listening to the fridge hum.

In Zurich there had always been someone. His mother on the phone in the kitchen, the neighbours' piano through the wall, the tram going past at the end of the street. Here there was the fridge, and the radiator ticking, and a view of the canal that was very nice in photos and very black at two in the morning.

He told himself he was fine. He was twenty-one. He had scored in Pittsburgh. He had a coffee machine that made seven kinds of coffee and he could not work any of them.

He got into bed with his glasses still on and opened Instagram, which was a mistake, because the first thing on his feed was the Centaurs' account, which meant Harris, which meant something wholesome designed to make him feel things at two in the morning.

He sent it, and then he read it back, and then he put his phone face down on the kitchen counter and stood very still with his hands over his face.

He had replied in one minute. One. At eight in the morning. Like he had been lying awake waiting for it, which he had not been, because he had been asleep with his glasses on.

The phone buzzed.

He did not look at it for a full thirty seconds, which was a personal record, and then it was only the shelter's automatic calendar invite, with a little cat paw next to the time.

The shelter smelled of disinfectant and wet dog and, faintly, of something baking next door. Luca stood at the front desk in his good jacket and his glasses, and the woman behind it looked up and said, "Oh, you're Gerald's," which nobody had ever called him before. He decided he liked it.

Mathis came out from the back carrying a clipboard and a towel, and Luca forgot, briefly, what he was there for.

He was maybe Luca's age, maybe a little older. Dark curly hair pushed back under a bandana covered in tiny cartoon fish. Scrubs, a cardigan over them with one pocket torn half off, and a long scratch on the back of one hand that he did not seem to have noticed. He had the kind of face that was already smiling before it got to you.

"Luca," he said. "Hi. I'm Mathis. We've, uh. Emailed."

"Yes," said Luca. "Hello. Yes. We have."

There was a pause in which neither of them said anything at all.

"Do you want to meet him?" Mathis said. "I should warn you, he's in a mood."

"What kind of mood?"

"Gerald only has one mood," Mathis said seriously, and led him down the corridor.

Gerald was in the last room on the left, on the top shelf of a cat tree that he had clearly conquered by force. He was even bigger in person. He was the colour of a carrot that had been left in the sun, with the one green eye and the torn ear, and he looked at Luca the way the Pittsburgh crowd had looked at him on Thursday after the goal.

"He's going to hiss," Mathis said. "Don't take it personally. He hisses at me every morning and I bring him breakfast."

Gerald hissed.

Luca sat down on the floor, because the shelter's website had said to get down at their level, and because his legs were not entirely working. He put his hand out on the lino, palm down, and waited.

Gerald thought about it for a long time. Then he climbed down the cat tree, one shelf at a time, like an old man going down the stairs. He walked past Luca's hand without looking at it. He turned around twice. And then he sat down, with great dignity, on both of Luca's feet.

Mathis made a noise like a kettle.

"He's never done that," he said. "He's never done that. I've been here every day for seven months and he's never sat on me. Not once."

"Maybe he likes hockey," Luca said, and Mathis laughed, a big surprised laugh that echoed off the walls, and Gerald flattened his one good ear and Luca felt his own face go hot all the way up to his hair.

"Oh nei. Ich bi verlore (Oh no. I'm doomed)," he said, under his breath.

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Luca said, "that he is very heavy."

Mathis looked at him a little longer than he needed to. "Sure," he said. "That's definitely what it means."

The paperwork took forty minutes, mostly because Luca had to write his own address three times. The first time he wrote his parents' address in Zurich. The second time he got the postcode wrong. Mathis watched him do it with his chin on his hand and did not say anything, which was somehow worse than if he had.

"He'll need a new name," Mathis said, while Luca was signing the last page. "If you want. Lots of people rename their fosters. We just call them whatever we call them when they come in, and he came in on a Tuesday and he looked like a Gerald."

Luca looked through the carrier door. Gerald looked back at him from inside, round and orange and furious, like something you would get in a café in Zurich on a Sunday with a fried egg on top.

"Rösti," he said.

"Rösti," said Mathis, trying it out. "Like the potato thing?"

"Like the potato thing."

Mathis grinned. "Perfect," he said, and wrote it on the carrier label in green marker, under Gerald, with a line through Gerald.

Rösti spent the first night under the bed and the second night under the sofa. On the third night Luca woke up at four in the morning with something extremely heavy on his chest, and opened his eyes to one green eye, very close, looking down at him with disgust.

He did not move until his alarm went off. It was the best night's sleep he had had since September.

On the morning of the 2018 draft he had changed the URL, locked the archive, and deleted the app. He had then reinstalled the app two days later, because he was weak, but now he posted only drawings, and pictures of the sky, and, as of the last two weeks, a lot of a large orange cat.
